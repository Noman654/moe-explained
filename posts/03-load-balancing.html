Load balancing

The loss that watches the router

Left alone, a router picks favourites and the rich get richer until most of your model is dead weight. The fix is four symbols long and almost everyone explains it wrong — including, for a while, me.

~18 min Assumes: chapter 2 Two animations, one exercise

The failure that doesn't crash

You have met this problem twice already. In chapter 1 you watched twelve tokens route through eight experts and end with E4 taking ten of them and E8 taking none. In chapter 2's exercise you found the reason nothing objected: the router's gates are smooth, but its dispatch is a cliff. You are in the top-k or you get nothing, and no term in the loss has any opinion about which.

Now consider what that costs. Mixtral holds 46.7B parameters and touches 12.9B per token. If three of its eight experts absorb nearly all the traffic, you are paying to store, shard, and move 45.1B parameters of experts while getting the representational benefit of roughly a third of them. You bought a sparse model and got an expensive dense one.

Why it compounds

Collapse is not a stumble the model recovers from. It is a positive feedback loop:

An expert that wins slightly more often → runs more often → receives more gradient → becomes genuinely better at the tokens it sees → earns a higher router score → wins more often still.

Meanwhile the loser runs rarely, receives almost no gradient, stays near its random initialisation, and therefore deserves its low score. The router isn't malfunctioning. It is correctly identifying that expert 8 is bad — at a problem it created by never training expert 8.

And here is what makes it genuinely dangerous rather than merely wasteful: nothing errors. No exception, no NaN, no alert. Training loss keeps falling. It just falls to a worse place than it should have, and you find out months later when your 47B model benchmarks like a 20B one.

Two fixes that don't work

Before the real answer, it is worth failing twice, because the failures explain the shape of the solution.

Attempt 1 — penalise the load directly

The obvious move: measure each expert's share of the tokens, call it fi, and add a penalty when it strays from 1/N.

It produces exactly zero gradient. fi is a count — how many tokens the argmax sent to expert i, divided by the batch. Counting involves a hard comparison, and hard comparisons have no derivative. You have written down a number that perfectly describes the problem and cannot be optimised.

Attempt 2 — penalise confident routing

So use something differentiable instead: the router's probabilities. Penalise Σ Pi2, which is minimised when the probabilities are uniform.

This has a gradient, and it aims at the wrong target. It punishes the router for being confident, everywhere, all the time — including on tokens where one expert genuinely is the right answer. It doesn't know which experts are overloaded because it never looks at the actual dispatch. You get a router that is uniformly wishy-washy and still imbalanced.

So: one term that knows the truth but can't be differentiated, and one that can be differentiated but doesn't know the truth.

Why f · P works when neither does alone

Multiply them together.

Switch Transformers, eq. 4
loss  =  α · N · Σi=1N  fi · Pi
Where fi is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (eq. 5) and Pi is the fraction of router probability allocated to expert i (eq. 6). Switch uses α = 10−2.

Now differentiate with respect to the only part that has a gradient. Since fi is a constant as far as autograd is concerned:

The whole trick, in one line
∂ loss∂Pi  =  α · N · fi
The downward pressure on an expert's routing probability is proportional to how overloaded that expert actually is. The non-differentiable term doesn't need a gradient — it acts as a per-expert weight telling the differentiable term how hard to push.

That is the entire idea, and it's why neither factor works alone. fi supplies the truth — real measured load. Pi supplies the gradient. The product routes the truth into the gradient without ever needing to differentiate through the counting.

The paper says so out loud

You don't have to take my derivation on faith — Switch states the split explicitly: “The objective can also be differentiated as the P-vector is differentiable, but the f-vector is not.”

GShard, arriving at the same problem a year earlier, made the same substitution for the same reason: it replaces a non-differentiable (ce/S)2 term with me · (ce/S), pairing a hard count with a soft mean gate. Two labs, two years apart, both landed on multiply the count you can't differentiate by the probability you can.

Which is a useful sanity check on how central this trick is: when independent groups converge on the same odd-looking product, it's usually because the constraint — not the taste — forced it.

Why the N out front

Under perfect balance every fi and Pi equals 1/N, so the sum is N · (1/N)(1/N) = 1/N. Multiplying by N makes the minimum value exactly 1, whatever N is — so α means the same thing whether you have 8 experts or 2048, and you don't have to retune it when you scale.

The other loss: bfloat16 is out to get you

Balance isn't the only way a router breaks. It is also, disproportionately, where training diverges — and the reason is numerical rather than statistical.

Research note

ST-MoE puts a number on it: bfloat16 has “up to 65,536x worse roundoff errors than float32.” And because the router's job ends in an exponential, those errors get amplified — the paper's own example is that a roundoff error of 0.5 in bfloat16 “will alter the softmax output by 36%.”

Sparse models are unusually exposed here because, as the paper puts it, they “introduce additional exponential functions—through the router—which can exacerbate roundoff errors.”

Large router logits make this worse, since exp of a large number is enormous and its representable neighbours are far apart. So ST-MoE adds a second auxiliary term whose only purpose is to keep the logits small:

ST-MoE, router z-loss
Lz(x)  =  1B Σi=1B  ( log Σj=1N exj(i) )2
Penalise the square of the log-sum-exp of the router logits, averaged over the B tokens. In plain terms: keep the router's raw scores small, so the exponential never has to represent a huge number. Coefficient cz = 0.001.

So a production MoE trains against three losses at once — the actual task, a term that keeps experts busy, and a term that keeps the arithmetic representable:

ST-MoE, total loss
Ltot = LCE + cB LB + cz LZ
Cross-entropy for the actual task, plus the balance loss, plus the z-loss. Two of the three exist purely to stop the router misbehaving.

Which should tell you something. Two-thirds of the loss function of a sparse model is devoted to babysitting a matrix that is one part in 44,538 of the parameters. That is the real cost of conditional computation, and it is not the one the headline numbers advertise.

Deleting the loss entirely

Every auxiliary loss is a compromise. It is a term you add to the objective that is not the thing you want — you want good language modelling, and you're spending gradient on load statistics. Push α too high and you damage the model to keep the histogram tidy; too low and it collapses anyway.

DeepSeek-V3's answer is to stop using a loss for this at all. Instead it adds a bias per expert, used only when deciding who wins:

DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2
g′i,t = si,t  if  si,t + bi ∈ TopK,   else 0
Look carefully at which side the bias is on. It appears inside the TopK test — it decides who gets selected. It does not appear in the value assigned to g′, which is the bare affinity si,t. The bias steers the choice and then gets out of the way.

The affinity itself is a sigmoid, not a softmax — each expert is scored independently — and the selected gates are normalised afterwards so they sum to 1, exactly the pattern chapter 2 predicted for k > 1:

DeepSeek-V3, affinity and normalisation
si,t = Sigmoid(utT ei)      gi,t = g′i,t Σj g′j,t
Score each expert independently with a sigmoid, select using score-plus-bias, then normalise the winners' unbiased scores so the gates sum to 1.

And the update rule is almost comically simple — no gradient involved at all. Per the paper, the bias is decreased by γ if the expert is overloaded and increased by γ if it is underloaded. It's a thermostat.

Why this is better than a loss

An auxiliary loss fights the task objective — both are pulling on the same weights, and balance is bought with model quality. The bias fights nothing. It isn't in the computational graph; it's a control signal adjusted outside the gradient. The task loss gets the whole gradient budget, and balance is handled by a separate mechanism that costs the model nothing.

And because the bias is excluded from the gate value, balancing never distorts how much a chosen expert contributes — only which experts get chosen. That separation is the whole design.

Note that last row, and note it carefully, because it is the most flattened fact in this whole subject: DeepSeek-V3 is not actually auxiliary-loss-free. In the paper's own words, it “mainly relies on the auxiliary-loss-free strategy for load balance,” but “to prevent extreme imbalance within any single sequence, we also employ a complementary sequence-wise balance loss.” A Switch-shaped term survives, scoped per sequence rather than per batch. The paper says its α “will be assigned an extremely small value” and never states the number — so this book won't either. It's a seatbelt, not a steering wheel. And γ itself is set to 0.001 for the first 14.3T tokens of training and then 0 for the remainder: once the experts have found their balance, the thermostat is switched off entirely.

Work it yourself

You're training an 8-expert MoE with Switch's auxiliary loss. After a few thousand steps the measured load is badly skewed:

f = [0.40, 0.02, 0.30, 0.05, 0.15, 0.03, 0.04, 0.01]

(a) With α = 10−2 and N = 8, what is ∂loss/∂Pi for the most-loaded expert versus the least-loaded one? What's the ratio?
(b) A colleague suggests fixing collapse faster by raising α to 1.0. What goes wrong?
(c) Under DeepSeek-V3's bias scheme instead, expert 1 is overloaded and expert 8 is starved. After 100 update steps, how far apart are their biases — and what does that do to a token where expert 1's affinity beats expert 8's by 0.05?

Show the answer

(a) ∂loss/∂Pi = α·N·fi = 0.01 × 8 × fi = 0.08 fi.
Expert 1: 0.08 × 0.40 = 0.032. Expert 8: 0.08 × 0.01 = 0.0008. The ratio is 40× — exactly the ratio of their loads, which is the point: the push-down is proportional to the offence.

(b) You'd be optimising the wrong objective. At α = 1.0 the balance term is comparable in magnitude to the cross-entropy loss, so gradient descent will happily hurt language modelling to flatten the histogram — the model learns to route uniformly whether or not uniform routing is right for the token. Perfectly balanced, measurably worse. This is precisely the compromise DeepSeek-V3's bias scheme exists to escape.

(c) Expert 1's bias falls by γ each step and expert 8's rises by γ, so after 100 steps they differ by 2 × 100 × 0.001 = 0.2 — assuming the imbalance persists that whole time. A 0.05 affinity lead is now comfortably overturned: expert 8 is selected. And critically, if expert 8 then wins the slot, its gate value is still its own unbiased affinity, because the bias never enters g′. Balance was corrected without lying to the model about how much it trusts expert 8.

The key insight in (c) is that the bias changes who runs, not how much their output counts. If you got that, you've got the chapter.

What you can now do

  • Explain why expert collapse is self-reinforcing, and why nothing errors.
  • Derive ∂loss/∂Pi = α·N·fi and say in one sentence why the product works when neither factor alone does.
  • Say why the N is out front.
  • Explain what the router z-loss protects against, and why bfloat16 makes a router riskier than a dense FFN.
  • Explain why a bias outside the gradient graph beats an auxiliary loss inside it — and why the bias must not touch the gate value.

Balance solves who should get the tokens. It says nothing about what happens when they arrive. Experts run on GPUs, GPUs demand fixed tensor shapes, and a fixed shape means a fixed number of seats. Balanced routing makes overflow rarer. It does not make it impossible — and what happens to a token that finds its expert full is the quietest failure in this entire architecture.
