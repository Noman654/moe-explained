4Chapter four · Capacity

The seat that wasn't there

Experts live in fixed-size buffers. Overflow one and your token skips the feed-forward network entirely and rides the residual to the next layer — silently, with no error. Then the papers report something genuinely surprising about how much that matters.

~17 min Assumes: chapter 3 Two animations, one exercise

Why there are buffers at all

Chapter 3 got the router to spread tokens evenly. You might reasonably think the problem is solved. It isn't, and the reason has nothing to do with machine learning.

GPUs and TPUs want static tensor shapes. Kernels are compiled against known dimensions; memory is allocated up front; collective communication operations need every participating device to agree on buffer sizes in advance. A layer that says "expert 3 will process however many tokens happen to want it this step" is not something you can compile efficiently, or at all.

So each expert is given a fixed number of seats, decided before the batch arrives:

Switch Transformers, eq. 3
expert capacity  =  tokens per batch number of experts  ×  capacity factor
Divide the batch evenly among the experts — that's the fair share — then multiply by a capacity factor, the slack you allow for imperfect balance. A factor of 1.0 is exactly the fair share and no more; 1.25 gives each expert a quarter more seats than it should need.
Feel it

It's a restaurant that must set out its chairs before knowing who's coming. Not "we'll grab more chairs if it's busy" — the chairs are bolted down at build time. Capacity factor is how many spare chairs you bolt down per table.

Where the analogy breaks: a real restaurant turns you away visibly and you go elsewhere. Here, as you're about to see, the diner walks through the restaurant, eats nothing, and leaves without anyone noticing.

The drop that doesn't raise

So what happens to the token that finds every seat taken? Both foundational papers describe it, in almost identical language.

Straight from the papers

Switch: “If too many tokens are routed to an expert (referred to later as dropped tokens), computation is skipped and the token representation is passed directly to the next layer through the residual connection.”

GShard puts the mechanism more precisely: the token “is considered as an overflowed token, where Gs,E degenerates into a zero vector. Such tokens have their representation xs passed on to the next layer via residual connections.”

Read GShard's version carefully, because it explains how rather than merely what. The gate vector goes to zero. Look back at the weighted sum from chapter 1 — if every gate is zero, the entire expert term contributes nothing, and the residual branch carries the token forward unchanged. No special case in the code. No branch. The token simply passes through the MoE layer as though it were the identity function.

A “dropped” token isn't deleted. It just misses one FFN's worth of thinking — and nothing anywhere logs that it happened.

The part almost every explainer gets wrong

Here is where the standard picture — one bucket per expert, filled from the whole batch — is not merely simplified but wrong, in a way that changes what you'd predict.

Capacity is enforced per local group, not globally. GShard makes this explicit as local group dispatching: the batch is split into G groups, each handled independently and in parallel, each expert getting a fractional capacity of 2N/(G·E) within that group. Switch's own appendix pseudocode computes capacity from tokens_per_core, not from the global batch.

The consequence is stark, and it's the thing to take from this chapter:

Why this matters practically

Global balance does not guarantee zero drops. Your router can be beautifully balanced across the whole batch — every expert at exactly 12.5% — and still drop tokens, because balance within each group is what capacity actually tests. The more groups you split across, the smaller each group, and the noisier its local distribution.

Which means capacity factor is not purely a modelling knob. It is coupled to how many devices you're sharding across, a fact that belongs as much to chapter 6 as to this one.

The surprise: lower was better

Intuition says a bigger capacity factor is safer — more slack, fewer drops, better model. Intuition is wrong, and Switch's Table 1 says so plainly.

Switch-Base at capacity factor 1.0 reaches the target quality in 62.8 hours at 1000 examples/sec. At capacity factor 2.0 it takes 72.8 hours at 860 examples/sec — 16% slower to get to the same place. The paper's own finding is blunt: “Switch Transformers perform better at lower capacity factors (1.0, 1.25).”

The reason is that capacity you don't use isn't free. Those seats are allocated memory and, critically, they are communicated — the all-to-all operations of chapter 6 move whole buffers, padding included. Doubling capacity doubles the padding you ship across the network to accomplish nothing.

Read the table honestly

Two caveats the paper flags itself, which anyone quoting this table should carry along.

First, the top-2 MoE baseline got slower going from capacity 2.0 to 1.25 (840 → 790 examples/sec), which is backwards. The authors footnote it: speed measurements are “both a function of the algorithm and the implementation details… the final speed differences are impacted by low-level optimizations.” This is not a clean monotonic curve and shouldn't be presented as one.

Second, drops were rare throughout: “typically <1%” of tokens, with no observed dependency on the number of experts. The capacity-factor result is mostly about wasted compute and communication, not about drop rate.

The obvious fix, which bought nothing

If dropping tokens is bad, catch them. Route an overflowed token to its second-choice expert; if that's full too, its third. Switch tried exactly this, under the name No-Token-Left-Behind, and it works mechanically — it “will allow us to guarantee almost no tokens will be dropped during training and inference.”

It did not help.

The negative result

“We hypothesised that this could improve performance and further stabilize training, but we found no empirical benefits. We suspect that once the network learns associations between different tokens and experts, if this association is changed (e.g. sending a token to its second highest expert) then performance could be degraded.”

That is worth sitting with, because it complicates the framing. A dropped token isn't simply lost value to be recovered at any cost — the obvious rescue bought nothing measurable.

Why not? The authors offer a hypothesis and explicitly label it as one: “We suspect that once the network learns associations between different tokens and experts, if this association is changed… then performance could be degraded.” The tempting extension — that skipping the FFN is a null operation while the wrong expert actively writes noise — is a reading I find persuasive, but it is my inference, not a measurement. What the paper establishes is only that rerouting didn't help.

GShard hedges differently: rather than a hard second choice, it dispatches to the runner-up stochastically, with probability proportional to that expert's gate weight (the condition 2·g₂ > rnd in its Algorithm 1). A token is only dropped when both its chosen experts are full. A pressure valve, not a guarantee.

Inverting the problem

Step back and notice the shape of everything so far. Tokens choose experts. Some experts get more tokens than they have seats. We then spend an auxiliary loss (chapter 3) and a capacity factor (this chapter) fighting the imbalance that choice creates.

What if the experts chose instead?

Expert Choice routing, eq. 2
S = Softmax(X · Wg)      G, I = TopK(S⊤, k)
The entire idea is that transpose. Ordinary routing takes the top-k across each token's row of expert scores; Expert Choice takes it down each expert's column instead, so every expert selects exactly k tokens — by construction, with no loss term required.

The bucket size follows directly:

Expert Choice, eq. 1
k  =  n × c e
For n tokens and e experts, each expert takes exactly k of them. Here c is a capacity factor meaning something different from Switch's — see the warning below.

The paper's claim is refreshingly flat: “Despite its simplicity, expert choice achieves perfect load balancing by design.” No auxiliary loss. No z-loss for balance. No capacity factor tuning to avoid drops. The imbalance problem that consumed all of chapter 3 is defined out of existence.

Two capacity factors, one name

This trips people constantly. In Switch, the capacity factor is slack on a buffer: 2.0 means each expert gets twice its fair share of seats. In Expert Choice, c is defined as “on average how many experts are utilized by a token”.

So Expert Choice at c = 2 is compute-matched to a top-2 model, and c = 1 to a top-1 model. They are not the same quantity, and comparing “capacity factor 2” across the two papers compares nothing.

What does Expert Choice give up? The symmetry it broke. Every expert gets exactly k tokens, but nothing now guarantees a token gets any experts. The paper is direct about the consequence: “each token can be routed to a variable number of experts and each expert can have a fixed bucket size.”

Where I'm inferring, and where the paper speaks

In the worked example above, token 5 is selected by no expert at all. That zero case is structurally possible — nothing in the formulation prevents it — but I want to be precise: the paper reports variable per-token expert counts and does not state how often a token receives zero. Treat the zero in my example as a demonstration of what the mechanism permits, not as a measured frequency.

The paper also offers an optional fix that summaries almost always omit (§3.3): an entropy-regularised linear program with a constraint capping each token's expert count, solved with Dykstra's algorithm. So “Expert Choice gives tokens unbounded compute” is only true of the default formulation.

And two deployment problems, both stated by the authors as limitations:

  • Autoregressive generation. “The expert choice method might not immediately apply to auto-regressive text generation as our current implementation takes in the past and future tokens to perform the top-k selection.” To pick its best k tokens, an expert must see the whole batch — so a token's routing depends on tokens that come after it. They propose grouping workarounds; it is a stated limitation, not a fatal flaw.
  • Small batches at serving time. With k = n·c/e, a small n collapses the bucket size. The authors suggest a global top-k with caps instead.

Work it yourself

You're training an MoE with 8 experts, a batch of 16,384 tokens, sharded across 8 cores (so 8 local groups of 2,048 tokens each), at capacity factor 1.0.

(a) What is each expert's capacity, per group?
(b) Your router is perfectly balanced globally — each expert gets exactly 1/8 of the full batch. In one particular group, expert 3 happens to attract 300 tokens. How many are dropped?
(c) Your colleague proposes raising the capacity factor to 2.0 to eliminate drops. Using Switch's Table 1 above, argue against it — and name the quantity that actually gets worse.

Show the answer

(a) Capacity is computed per group, not per batch: 2048 / 8 × 1.0 = 256 seats per expert per group. (Compute it from the global batch and you'd wrongly get 2,048 — eight times too many — and conclude drops were impossible.)

(b) 300 − 256 = 44 tokens dropped, in that group, despite the router being perfectly balanced across the batch as a whole. This is the chapter's central point: global balance does not prevent local overflow. Those 44 tokens skip the FFN and ride the residual, and nothing logs it.

(c) Doubling capacity to 2.0 gives 512 seats and would indeed absorb the 44. But Switch-Base at CF 2.0 took 72.8 hours to reach the quality threshold versus 62.8 hours at CF 1.0, and ran at 860 vs 1000 examples/sec — 16% slower to the same quality. The quantity that gets worse is throughput, because unused seats are still allocated and still shipped: the all-to-all collectives move whole buffers, padding included. You'd be paying a permanent 14% speed tax on every step to rescue <1% of tokens — and per No-Token-Left-Behind, rescuing them wasn't worth much anyway.

Full credit on (b) if you divided by the group, not the batch. That single distinction is what this chapter is for.

And then the frontier walked away from the problem

One caveat on everything above, and it is a big one: DeepSeek-V3 has no capacity factor and drops no tokens, in training or inference. There is no fixed buffer to overflow.

It gets there by pairing the bias-based balancing of chapter 3 with, at serving time, redundant experts — duplicating high-load experts and rebalancing the duplicates roughly every ten minutes. Balance good enough to make buffers unnecessary, plus operational slack where balance still fails.

So read this chapter as describing the GShard–Switch lineage, which is most of what's deployed and nearly all of what's written about. It is not a universal law of Mixture-of-Experts, and a 2026 reader should know which generation they're looking at.

What you can now do

  • Compute expert capacity, and know to divide by the group, not the global batch.
  • Explain exactly what happens to a dropped token — the gate goes to zero and the residual carries it — and why nothing errors.
  • Explain why global balance still permits local drops.
  • Say why lower capacity factors turned out to train faster.
  • Explain Expert Choice's transpose in one sentence, and name what it trades away.
  • Avoid the trap of comparing "capacity factor" across papers that define it differently.
  • Say which generation of MoE actually has a capacity factor at all.

We now have the whole machine: conditional computation, a router, a loss to keep it honest, and buffers to make it run on real hardware. Every piece arrived as a response to a specific failure of the piece before it. Which makes it worth going back to the beginning and reading the seven-year argument in order — because the sequence explains the design far better than any of the papers do alone.
