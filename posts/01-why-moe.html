1Chapter one · Foundations

Every token pays full price

Why Mixture-of-Experts exists, and how a 47-billion-parameter model does 13 billion parameters of work. By the end you'll derive Mixtral's spec sheet from ten numbers — and read any MoE paper's headline without flinching.

~16 min Assumes: you know what a transformer FFN is Everything else is built up from there

Type the word “the” into a language model. Now type “photosynthesis”.

The model does exactly the same amount of arithmetic for both.

Same matrices. Same multiply-accumulates. Same joules. One of those tokens is a grammatical shrug the model could predict in its sleep; the other drags in chloroplasts and light-dependent reactions and a whole subfield of biology. The model can't tell the difference, because it has exactly one brain and it runs all of it, every single time.

That's a dense model. And once you notice how strange it is, Mixture-of-Experts stops looking like an exotic trick and starts looking like the obvious thing to try.

Feel it

A dense model is a hospital where every patient is seen by every doctor. Cardiology, dermatology, oncology — all of them, for a sprained ankle. MoE adds a triage desk that sends you to the two doctors you actually need.

Where the analogy breaks: the hospital still pays every doctor's salary whether they see you or not. That single caveat is the entire subject of chapter 6 — and it's why MoE is a training-cost win but an inference-memory problem.

See it happen first

Before any equations, watch one token go through. Everything in this chapter is a footnote to these eight stages — press play, or step through at your own pace.

Two things in that sequence do all the work, and both are easy to miss. The softmax happens after the cut, over only the two survivors — which is why the gates sum to 1 across two experts rather than eight. And the six losers aren't multiplied by zero; they are never computed at all. That's not an optimisation detail. That's the entire idea.

Where the parameters actually live

To see why the FFN is the thing worth attacking, count. A transformer block has two halves — attention and a feed-forward network — and they are not remotely the same size.

Take Mixtral 8×7B's published config: model dimension dmodel = 4096, FFN hidden dimension dff = 14336, 32 attention heads of dimension 128, but only 8 key/value heads (grouped-query attention).

Why Mixtral, specifically

Because it's the one you can check. Mixtral's Table 1 publishes every number needed to reconstruct its headline figures, and those figures reconcile to within a rounding step — rarer than it ought to be. The weights are open, so you can load it and verify me.

And architecturally it is the plainest possible MoE: eight equal experts, top-2, and nothing else. No shared expert, no fine-grained segmentation, no bias-based balancing, no node-limited routing. Every complication this book introduces later is absent here, which is exactly what you want from a base case.

What Mixtral is not is current practice. Its 8-experts-top-2 shape is the 2024 design; the field has since moved to many-small-experts — DeepSeek-V3 runs 256. Treat it as the skeleton, not the state of the art. Chapter 5 is the story of why it changed.

The FFN is SwiGLU, so it's three matrices, not two — a gate projection and an up projection going in, a down projection coming out:

FFN params
3 × dmodel × dff  =  3 × 4096 × 14336  =  176,160,768
One feed-forward network in this model holds 176 million parameters. Three matrices, each 4096 × 14336.

Now attention. Query and output projections are full-size; key and value projections are shrunk 4× by GQA:

Attention params
2 × (4096×4096)Q,O  +  2 × (4096×1024)K,V  =  41,943,040
All of attention in one layer is 42 million parameters — less than a quarter of what the single FFN next to it costs.

At these dimensions a single FFN is 81% of a dense block's parameters. If you want to make a transformer bigger, the FFN is where the size lives — so it's also the only place worth being clever.

(Hold onto that “dense” qualifier. Once we replace that one FFN with eight, the expert block swells to 97% of the layer — which is exactly why everything from here on is about managing experts, and almost nothing is about attention.)

The dense wall

So you want a smarter model. The reliable lever is more parameters, and the cheapest place to put them is dff. Double it and the model gets meaningfully better.

It also gets exactly twice as expensive. Every token, every layer, every forward pass, forever. In a dense network parameters and arithmetic are welded together: one more parameter is one more multiply-accumulate for every token that ever passes through. Training cost, inference cost, and latency all rise in lockstep with capacity.

That's the wall. Capacity is what you want; compute is what you pay; and in a dense model there's no exchange rate between them — they're the same number wearing two hats.

The move

Conditional computation: break the weld. Store a huge number of parameters, but for any given token run only a small slice of them. Capacity scales with what you store; cost scales with what you touch. Make those two numbers different and you can buy capacity without buying FLOPs.

The idea is old — Jacobs and Hinton were writing about mixtures of local experts in 1991 — but it only became load-bearing for language models when Shazeer et al. (2017) bolted a sparsely-gated MoE layer between LSTM layers and built one containing up to 137 billion parameters in the MoE layer alone, in an era when “large” meant a few hundred million. (That figure is the layer, not the whole model — and as chapter 5 covers, it was also the configuration where quality started going backwards.)

What an MoE layer actually is

Here's the entire architectural change, and it's smaller than you'd expect:

Take the FFN. Make N copies of it. Add one small matrix that scores which copies to use. Run only the top k, and add their outputs together, weighted by their scores.

That's it. Attention is untouched. The residual stream is untouched. The only thing that changed is that one FFN became N FFNs plus a router.

The math

Formally, with N experts E1..N and a gating network G, the layer output is a weighted sum — equation 1 of Shazeer et al. (2017), and it hasn't changed since:

Shazeer 2017, eq. 1
y  =  Σi=1N  G(x)i  ·  Ei(x)
The output is every expert's answer, blended by how much the router liked that expert. The trick is that G(x) is sparse — all but k entries are exactly zero, so all but k of the Ei(x) terms never have to be computed at all.

That last sentence is the whole engineering payoff. The sum is written over all N experts, but a zero gate is a term you can skip — you don't multiply by zero, you never do the multiply. Mathematically it's a sum over N; computationally it's a sum over k.

Mixtral instantiates this with N = 8, k = 2, and SwiGLU experts:

Mixtral, §2.1
y  =  Σi=0n−1  Softmax(Top2(x · Wg))i  ·  SwiGLUi(x)
Score all 8 experts with one small matrix Wg, keep the best 2, softmax those two scores only, and use them to blend the two experts' outputs — exactly stages 2 to 6 of the animation above. Why softmax comes after the top-k is chapter 2.

Routing is per-token — and that's where the trouble starts

The router runs independently for every single token. Not per sentence, not per document — per token, at every MoE layer. So watch what happens when a real sentence flows through and we keep a tally underneath each expert.

The problem you just watched

Nothing in the architecture says the load has to be even. The router is trained, it develops preferences, and preferences compound: an expert that gets more tokens gets more gradient, gets better, and gets picked more. Meanwhile expert 8 is 176 million parameters sitting in expensive memory contributing literally nothing.

That's expert collapse, and it is the single biggest thing standing between “MoE works on paper” and “MoE works.” All of chapter 3 is about the loss term that fixes it.

Derive the spec sheet yourself

Mixtral is sold as “47B total, 13B active.” Those aren't marketing numbers — they fall out of the config table, and you can reconstruct them in about six lines. Do this once and every MoE announcement becomes legible.

You need ten numbers, all from the paper's Table 1:

# Mixtral 8x7B — every number from Table 1, nothing else
d_model, d_ff, layers, vocab = 4096, 14336, 32, 32000
n_experts, top_k = 8, 2
kv_dim = 8 * 128       # 1024 — GQA shrinks K,V 4x

# one SwiGLU expert is 3 matrices; attention is Q,O full + K,V shrunk
expert = 3 * d_model * d_ff              # 176,160,768
attn   = 2*d_model**2 + 2*d_model*kv_dim  #  41,943,040
embed  = 2 * vocab * d_model           # 262,144,000

total  = layers * (n_experts * expert + attn) + embed
active = layers * (top_k     * expert + attn) + embed

print(total, active, total / active)
# 46,701,477,888   12,878,610,432   3.63

Both land on the published figures. And notice the shape of the arithmetic: the experts are the only term n_experts multiplies. Attention and embeddings appear identically in both lines, which is why they're “free” in the sparsity ratio and why the ratio (3.63×) is lower than the naive 8 ÷ 2 = 4× you'd expect. The non-expert parameters dilute it.

Work it yourself

DeepSeek-V3 is 671B total, 37B active — an 18.1× sparsity ratio, five times sparser than Mixtral. It uses 256 routed experts with top-8 routing, plus 1 shared expert that runs for every token.

Question: naively you'd expect a ratio near 256 ÷ 9 ≈ 28×. The real figure is 18.1×. Without looking anything up, name the two effects that account for the gap — and say which one you'd expect to dominate at this scale.

Show the answer

1 — the non-expert floor. Attention, embeddings, and the shared expert are paid in full on every token. They sit in the numerator and the denominator, so they drag any sparsity ratio toward 1×. This is the same dilution that pulled Mixtral from 4× down to 3.63× — a 9% haircut.

2 — the shared expert isn't optional. Of the ~9 experts active per token, 1 is shared and always on. It contributes to active params while contributing only 1/257th of total. It behaves like attention, not like a routed expert.

Which dominates: the non-expert floor, and it isn't close. DeepSeek-V3 uses Multi-head Latent Attention over 61 layers with a 129k vocab — the attention and embedding block is large in absolute terms, and unlike the experts it gets no sparsity discount at all. The lesson generalises: as you crank the expert count, the dense remainder becomes the binding constraint on your sparsity ratio. You can't route your way out of attention.

Full credit if you said “the stuff that isn't experts” and “the shared expert always fires.” The exact split needs numbers we haven't given you.

Dashes are honest. The Switch and Shazeer papers report total parameters and expert counts but no directly comparable “active parameters per token” figure, so this book won't invent one. Shazeer's 137B is the size of the MoE layer, not of a whole model, and its top-k was 4 — the large configurations were hierarchical (a gate over gates), not a flat top-k over 131,072 experts.

Research note

Switch Transformer reports up to a 7× pre-training speedup over T5-Base, and Switch-C reaching a fixed perplexity 4× faster than T5-XXL. Those two numbers mean different things, and it matters.

The 7× is at equal FLOPs per token — Switch-Base is explicitly FLOP-matched to T5-Base. The 4× is at equal training budget: Switch-C actually applies far fewer FLOPs per sequence than T5-XXL (890B vs 6.3T, Table 9). It is the FLOP-matched Switch-XXL, not Switch-C, that compares like for like. Neither number claims the model is smaller, cheaper to serve, or easier to train — two of those three are in fact worse.

The bill

A book that only sold you the upside would be a brochure. Conditional computation buys capacity per FLOP, and it pays in four currencies:

What it costs

1 · Memory doesn't get the discount. Every one of Mixtral's 46.7B parameters must sit in VRAM, because the router might ask for any of them on the next token. You buy compute like a 13B model and RAM like a 47B one. → chapter 6

2 · Routers pick favourites. You watched it happen above. Left alone, a router funnels everything into a handful of experts and the rest atrophy into dead weight you still pay to store. → chapter 3

3 · Experts have fixed-size buffers. GPUs need static shapes, so each expert gets a capacity. Overflow it and your token is silently dropped — it skips the FFN and rides the residual to the next layer. Nothing errors. The loss just gets a bit worse. → chapter 4

4 · Experts don't fit on one GPU. So routing becomes a network operation: two all-to-all collectives per MoE layer, in the critical path of every forward pass. → chapter 6

None of these are dealbreakers — DeepSeek-V3 trained on 14.8 trillion tokens for 2.788M H800 GPU-hours without a single irrecoverable loss spike, a remarkable stability result for an architecture with this many moving parts. But every one is a real engineering cost, and the next five chapters are largely about how the field learned to pay them.

One last thing, because it's not what you'd guess

The name “expert” invites an assumption: expert 3 handles code, expert 5 handles French, expert 7 does arithmetic. It's the natural reading, it's what the hospital analogy implies, and it is — as far as anyone has been able to measure — wrong.

Research note

Mixtral's authors went looking for exactly this specialisation and reported: “Surprisingly, we do not observe obvious patterns in the assignment of experts based on the topic.” Routing distributions for arXiv papers, biology abstracts, and philosophy text looked much the same. Only DM Mathematics stood out, most likely because its synthetic templates make it lexically peculiar.

What they did find was syntactic structure — the token self in Python and the token Question in English each route consistently, regardless of surrounding topic — and strong positional locality. Read Table 5 carefully, because it holds two different measures: at layer 15, 24–28% of consecutive tokens get the same top-1 expert, against the paper's stated 12.5% random baseline — while 62–67% share an expert among either of their two choices, against the ~46% baseline the paper gives for that measure. Pairing one column's numbers with the other's baseline is an easy mistake, and it inflates a real effect into an implausible one.

So experts specialise, but by token-level surface structure, not by subject. Keep that in your pocket — it's the fact that makes DeepSeekMoE's fine-grained-expert argument in chapter 5 click into place.

What you can now do

  • Explain why the FFN, not attention, is the thing MoE replaces — with the parameter counts to back it up.
  • Read “671B total / 37B active” and know precisely what was traded for what.
  • Derive a published MoE model's headline numbers from its config table.
  • Explain why sparsity ratios never reach N ÷ k.
  • Name the four costs, and which chapter explains each.

What we've waved at so far is the router — stages 2 through 4 of that first animation went by fast, and “picks the top 2” hid the hard part. The router has to make a discrete choice while staying differentiable enough to train, and the tension between those two demands explains nearly every design decision in the rest of this book.
