2Chapter two · The router

The router's impossible job

It has to make a discrete choice — this expert, not that one — while staying differentiable enough to train. Those two demands are in direct conflict, and the resolution explains almost every design decision in the rest of this book.

~15 min Assumes: chapter 1 Two animations, one exercise

One part in forty-four thousand

Before anything else, a sense of scale. Mixtral's router is a single matrix per layer, mapping the model dimension to the number of experts:

Router size
dmodel × N × layers  =  4096 × 8 × 32  =  1,048,576
Every router in the entire model, added together, is about one million parameters — against 46.7 billion in the model as a whole. That's one part in 44,537, or 0.0022%.

The router is a rounding error in the parameter count and it decides where everything goes. Get it wrong and the other 99.998% of the model is arranged in the wrong order.

Feel it

It's the switchboard operator, not the switchboard. Nothing important happens in the router — no reasoning, no features, no knowledge. All it does is point. But everything downstream depends on it pointing well, and a switchboard operator who sends every call to the same three lines makes the other lines useless no matter how good they are.

Where the analogy breaks: a human operator knows who's busy. This one is a single matrix multiply with no memory of what it just did and no idea what the other tokens in the batch are doing. Making it aware of load is chapter 3.

The job, stated precisely

The router receives one token's hidden state — a vector of 4096 numbers — and must produce a decision: which of the 8 experts should run, and how much should each of their outputs count.

Step one is unremarkable. Multiply by a matrix, softmax the result:

Shazeer 2017, softmax gating
Gσ(x)  =  Softmax(x · Wg)
Multiply the token by the router matrix to get one score per expert, then squash those scores into positive numbers that sum to 1. Nothing exotic — this is the same operation as the output layer of any classifier.

Step two is where it gets hard. We don't want all eight probabilities — the entire point of chapter 1 was to avoid running all eight experts. We want to pick k of them and ignore the rest.

And picking is not a differentiable operation.

The conflict

Gradient descent needs continuity. It learns by asking "if I nudge this weight a hair, how does the loss change?" But top-k is a step function: nudge a router weight by 0.001 and either nothing changes (same experts win) or the selection flips entirely to a different expert. The derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jumps.

So the router must make a hard choice, and hard choices have no gradient. That's the impossible job in one sentence.

Why the gate has to multiply the output

Here is the resolution, and it is genuinely clever precisely because it looks like nothing at all. Recall the layer output from chapter 1:

Shazeer 2017, eq. 1
y  =  Σi  G(x)i  ·  Ei(x)
The gate multiplies the expert's output rather than merely selecting it. That multiplication is the only reason the router can be trained at all.

Differentiate. The gradient of the loss with respect to a gate value is

The gradient path
∂y∂Gi  =  Ei(x)    ⇒    ∂L∂Wg  ≠  0
Because y depends on the gate, and the gate depends on Wg, gradient flows back into the router. And notice what it receives: a signal proportional to the expert's own output. The router is literally told “how much did routing here help?”

Now delete the gate. Suppose the layer just ran the winning expert directly, y = Etop(x), with no weighting. The output no longer contains Wg anywhere. The derivative is exactly zero. The router would receive no gradient, ever, and would never improve on its random initialisation.

The math, stated honestly

That derivation is calculus on equation 1, not a sentence quoted from a paper — I'm flagging it because this book's rule is that you always know which you're reading. The equation is Shazeer's; the consequence is what falls out of differentiating it, and you can check the step yourself in one line.

The practical upshot is a rule you'll see obeyed in every MoE paper since: the top-k selection is non-differentiable and stays that way. The gradient never flows through the choice. It flows through the weight attached to the choice. The router learns to assign better magnitudes, and better rankings come along for the ride.

The ordering nobody mentions

So we softmax, and we take the top k. In which order?

It sounds like a pedantic question. It is not. Switch and Mixtral answer it differently, and their published equations say so plainly — but almost every explainer flattens the two into one.

Switch, eq. 1 — softmax first
pi(x)  =  eh(x)i ΣjN eh(x)j
The denominator runs over all N experts. Switch computes the full distribution, then takes the single largest.
Mixtral, §2.1 — top-k first
Softmax(Top2(x · Wg))
Read it inside-out: Top2 runs first, and the softmax is applied to what survives. The denominator has two terms, not eight.

Same eight numbers going in. Different denominators. Which means different gate values on the very same expert — watch:

Read that number again

Expert 3 wins under both schemes. Under Switch's ordering its gate is 0.432; under Mixtral's it is 0.668 — 55% larger, from identical logits. The expert's output gets scaled by that gate before it enters the residual stream, so this is not bookkeeping. It changes the activations, and it changes the gradient the router receives.

Why does each paper choose as it does? The honest answer is that the papers don't frame it as a debate, so what follows is the structural argument rather than a quote:

  • Softmax-over-all (Switch) keeps the gate calibrated against the full field. A gate of 0.43 genuinely means “the router was 43% confident in this expert out of everything available.” When the router is torn between three experts, the winner's gate is small, and the layer's contribution shrinks accordingly. Uncertainty naturally damps the output.
  • Softmax-over-survivors (Mixtral) guarantees the gates sum to 1. With top-2, the two gates always total exactly 1.0, so the layer's output is a proper weighted average of two experts and its magnitude doesn't fluctuate with how confident the router was. That's a real stability property when k > 1 — and with k = 1, as in Switch, renormalising would force every gate to exactly 1.0 and destroy the confidence signal entirely.

Which explains a pattern you'll see often, though not universally: top-1 architectures tend to softmax over everything; top-k>1 architectures often normalise over the survivors. It is a tendency, not a rule — DeepSeekMoE activates 6 routed experts yet softmaxes over all of them and uses the surviving scores without renormalising, which is the Switch ordering at k > 1. DeepSeek-V3 follows the same logic, normalising its 8 selected gates so they sum to 1 (we'll do that equation properly in chapter 3, because its selection rule has a twist).

Noisy top-k, and what the noise is for

Shazeer's original router adds something that looks like sabotage: random noise, injected before the selection.

Shazeer 2017, noisy top-k
H(x)i  =  (x · Wg)i  +  StandardNormal() · Softplus((x · Wnoise)i)
Add Gaussian noise to each expert's score before ranking — and note the noise amount is itself learned, through a second matrix Wnoise, passed through Softplus to keep it positive. The model decides per-token how much randomness to inject.

Then the selection and softmax, with everything outside the top k sent to negative infinity so the softmax zeroes it:

Shazeer 2017, KeepTopK
KeepTopK(v, k)i  =  vi  if in top k,   −∞ otherwise
Setting a logit to −∞ makes its ex exactly zero, so the losing experts drop out of the softmax cleanly — and their terms vanish from the sum, which is what lets you skip computing them.

So why deliberately corrupt the scores? The paper's own answer is narrower and more interesting than the one usually given. §2.1 says only that “the noise term helps with load balancing, as will be discussed in Appendix A” — and Appendix A gives the mechanism: the noise is what makes Load(X) a smooth, differentiable estimator of how many examples each expert receives. Without randomness, that load is a hard count with no gradient (the exact problem chapter 3 is built around). With it, you can write a load-balancing loss you can actually backpropagate.

My gloss, labelled as such

Noise is often explained as exploration — letting an underdog expert occasionally win so it can earn the gradient it needs to improve. That reading is intuitive and probably part of the story, but I want to be clear it is my gloss, not Shazeer's claim: the paper's stated purpose is differentiable load estimation, and it never frames the noise as exploration.

What routing actually looks like

Enough theory — what does a trained router do? Mixtral's authors measured it, and the results are not what the word “expert” leads you to expect.

Research note

They looked for topic specialisation and reported: “Surprisingly, we do not observe obvious patterns in the assignment of experts based on the topic.” arXiv papers, biology abstracts, and philosophy routed much alike. Only DM Mathematics stood apart, most likely because its synthetic templates are lexically peculiar rather than because it is mathematical.

What they did find was syntactic: the token self in Python and Question in English each route consistently regardless of surrounding topic. And positional locality. Table 5 reports two distinct measures and it is worth keeping them apart: at layer 15, 24–28% of consecutive tokens share the same top-1 expert, against the paper's stated 12.5% random baseline — and 62–67% share an expert as either first or second choice, against the ~46% baseline the paper gives for that one.

Those ratios deserve a moment. Top-1 repetition runs about 1.9–2.3× chance, and first-or-second overlap about 1.3–1.4×. Modest multiples — but a real, consistent correlation along the sequence, which is very good news for systems people: expert activations have temporal locality you can cache and batch against. It's why chapter 6 can say anything useful about MoE inference at all.

It also reframes the word “expert.” These aren't specialists in subjects. They're specialists in token shapes: this expert handles code punctuation, that one handles the tails of English function words. Which is precisely why cutting experts smaller and letting a token combine more of them turns out to work — the argument DeepSeekMoE makes in chapter 5.

Work it yourself

A router produces these logits for four experts:

E1 = 3.0    E2 = 2.9    E3 = 0.1    E4 = -0.5

The architecture uses top-2 routing.

(a) Under Mixtral's ordering (top-k, then softmax over the survivors), what gates do E1 and E2 receive?
(b) Now suppose the token shifts slightly and E2's logit rises to 3.1, overtaking E1. How much do the two gates change?
(c) What does your answer to (b) tell you about how much the router's ranking matters versus its margins — and why might that be a problem for load balancing?

Show the answer

(a) Softmax over {3.0, 2.9}: e³·⁰ = 20.086, e²·⁹ = 18.174, sum 38.260. So E1 = 0.525, E2 = 0.475. Nearly an even split, because the logits are nearly equal.

(b) Softmax over {3.0, 3.1}: e³·⁰ = 20.086, e³·¹ = 22.198, sum 42.284. Now E1 = 0.475, E2 = 0.525. The gates swapped, but each moved only 0.05. The output of the layer barely changed — it was ~52/48 of the same two experts before and is ~48/52 now.

(c) Two lessons. First, when the top-k margin is thin the exact ranking hardly matters to the output: you get a near-average of both experts either way, so the layer is robust to router jitter. That's a feature.

Second — and this is the problem — the same is not true of load. E3 and E4 got gate 0 in both cases; they were never even candidates, and a logit of 0.1 versus 3.0 is a gulf no jitter will close. The router's output is smooth, but its dispatch is a hard cliff: you are either in the top-2 or you receive nothing at all. A tiny, permanent preference for E1 and E2 starves E3 and E4 completely, and nothing in the loss so far has noticed. That gap is exactly what chapter 3 closes.

Credit for (c) if you spotted that smooth gates and hard dispatch are different things.

What you can now do

  • Explain why the gate must multiply the expert output, and what breaks if it doesn't — in one line of calculus.
  • Spot whether a paper softmaxes before or after its top-k, and say what that does to the denominator and the gate.
  • Explain why top-1 architectures normalise over all experts while top-k>1 architectures normalise over the survivors.
  • Say what the noise in noisy top-k is for.
  • Describe what real experts specialise in — and it isn't topics.

Everything so far has treated the router as an isolated decision-maker: one token arrives, one choice comes out. But a router serves a whole batch, and its choices across that batch interact. Left to itself it will discover a handful of favourites and ride them — you watched it happen in chapter 1, and the exercise above showed why nothing in the loss objects. Fixing that requires adding a term whose only job is to make the router care about a quantity it cannot see.
