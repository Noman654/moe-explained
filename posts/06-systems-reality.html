6Chapter six · Systems

When the network becomes the model

Your experts don't fit on one accelerator, so routing stops being arithmetic and becomes a communication problem. This is the chapter the architecture papers skip — and the one that decides whether any of the previous five were worth it.

~18 min Assumes: chapters 1–5 Two animations, one exercise

Everything so far has quietly assumed the model sits somewhere you can reach. It doesn't. DeepSeek-V3's 671B parameters at BF16 are 1.34 TB; even at FP8 they are 671 GB, against an H800's 80. The experts must be spread across devices. And the instant they are, the router's output stops being an index and becomes a destination.

Expert parallelism

If you have never sharded a model, start here

The rest of this chapter assumes three ways of splitting one computation across many devices. They take a paragraph.

Data parallelism (DP) — every device holds the whole model and a different slice of the batch. Cheap and obvious; it fails the moment the model stops fitting on one device.

Tensor parallelism (TP) — one matrix is cut into pieces across devices, each computing part of every multiply. The pieces must be stitched back together constantly, so TP wants a very fast interconnect.

Pipeline parallelism (PP) — different layers live on different devices, and activations flow down the chain like a factory line.

A collective is an operation every device participates in at once. Two matter here: an all-reduce (everyone contributes a value, everyone gets the total — how DP shares gradients) and an all-to-all (everyone sends a different piece to everyone else — the one MoE needs). And when you see a config written PP16 × EP64, read it as letter = which kind, number = across how many devices.

With those in hand: MoE adds a third axis that is peculiar to it.

Expert parallelism: different experts live on different devices. Nothing is split — expert 7 sits whole on GPU 3, and if your token wants expert 7, your token must go to GPU 3.

This is a good deal. Experts are the bulk of the parameters (chapter 1: 97% of an MoE block), they're independent, and they never need to talk to each other. Splitting by expert is the natural cut. The bill arrives at dispatch time.

Feel it

Tensor parallelism is four people each doing a quarter of every task. Expert parallelism is four specialists in four different buildings — nobody duplicates work, but every job has to be couriered to the right building and the result couriered back.

Where the analogy breaks: the couriers all leave at once and nobody starts work until every parcel has arrived. That synchrony is what makes the communication a wall rather than a delay.

The two all-to-alls

Each device holds a slice of the batch and a subset of the experts. The tokens on your device mostly want experts that are somewhere else, and the tokens that want your experts are mostly on other devices. So every device must send something to every other device, simultaneously. That collective is an all-to-all, and an MoE layer needs two of them.

Two, and the cleanest source is DeepSeek-V3

DeepSeek-V3 §3.2.1 names all four pieces of an MoE layer's work: “we divide each chunk into four components: attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP, and all-to-all combine.”

GShard's Algorithm 2 shows the same thing structurally — two einsums that flip which dimension is sharded, group → expert on the way in and expert → group on the way out.

And a correction to the usual phrasing: it's two all-to-alls per layer per pass. DualPipe splits forward and backward chunks the same way, so a training step pays four per MoE layer. Explainers routinely say “two per layer” and leave the backward pass implicit.

What it actually costs

GShard measured this, and the numbers are the best argument in the literature for why expert count isn't free.

Roofline, in one sentence

The roofline is the ceiling on what a chip can actually deliver: for any given piece of work you are limited either by how fast it computes or by how fast it can feed itself data, whichever binds first. “48% of roofline” means the hardware achieved under half of what it was capable of on that workload — the rest was spent waiting. Machine balance, which the calculator later in this chapter uses, is the crossover point between those two regimes, measured in FLOPs per byte moved.

All-to-all (dispatch + combine) as a share of layer time16%36%

Gating overhead (ArgMax, Cumsum)negligible<10%

Going 16× wider in experts, the all-to-all share of runtime more than doubles and hardware utilisation falls from over 70% of roofline to under half. This is the systems half of the story chapter 5 told from the modelling side. Though be careful attributing the Switch-C/Switch-XXL gap to communication: the paper itself explains it by compute per token — “the Switch-XXL model applies ~10x the FLOPS per token than the Switch-C model” — and attaches its communication caveat to Switch-XXL, not Switch-C. What you're looking at here is why 2048 experts are expensive to run, which is a related but separate claim.

The scaling law, and its fine print

GShard derives all-to-all cost on their cluster as O(√D) in the number of partitions D — growing partitions 128× (16 → 2048) increased all-to-all time only 9×, and √128 ≈ 11.3, so the fit is good.

But read the qualifier: the derivation is “on our 2D TPU cluster,” from h = O(√D) average hops on a 2D mesh with l = O(D) links. It is a property of that topology, not a law of nature — on a fat-tree GPU cluster the hop-count assumption doesn't hold. Compare AllReduce on the same hardware, which the paper measures as roughly O(1), independent of device count. All-to-all is the expensive collective, and MoE is the architecture that needs it.

How bad is it left alone? DeepSeek-V3 is blunt: cross-node expert parallelism gives “an inefficient computation-to-communication ratio of approximately 1:1”. Half your cluster-time moving bytes.

Their fix, DualPipe, overlaps the communication with computation so that “both all-to-all and PP communication can be fully hidden during execution.” Hidden, not eliminated — and not free either: the scheme keeps two copies of the model parameters, which the paper justifies only “since we use a large EP size during training.”

Why exactly four nodes

DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to at most 4 nodes. This gets described everywhere as a way of limiting how many experts a token uses. That has the mechanism backwards — the expert count is already fixed at 8 by top-k. The constraint is about bandwidth.

The ratio that sets M = 4
NVLink 160 GB/sInfiniBand 50 GB/s  ≈  3.2
Inside a node, GPUs talk over NVLink at roughly 3.2× the bandwidth of the InfiniBand fabric between nodes. So the design question isn't "how many experts?" but "how many times must this token cross the slow link?"

The routing then works in two hops. A token crosses InfiniBand once per target node, landing on the GPU with the same in-node index, then reaches its actual expert over the much faster NVLink. Cap the nodes at 4 and you cap the expensive traffic at 4 crossings regardless of which 8 experts were chosen.

And the 3.2 tells you the headroom: the paper notes that its 8 routed experts could grow to 13 (4 × 3.2) at unchanged communication cost. The architecture is sized to the interconnect, not the other way round.

How the four nodes get picked

Not by "the node with the best single expert," and not by average affinity. Nodes are ranked by the sum of the highest Kr/M affinity scores of the experts on each node — with Kr = 8 and M = 4, that's the sum of each node's top two experts. It asks "which nodes can give me a good pair?", which is the right question when you'll be taking roughly two experts per node.

The float32 island

Chapter 3 covered why routers are numerically fragile in bfloat16. Switch's fix is more interesting than "use float32," and the reason is communication.

PrecisionQuality (neg. log perp.)Speed (examples/sec)
float32 throughout−1.7181160
bfloat16 throughout−3.780 diverged1390
Selective precision−1.7161390

Switch Transformers, selective precision ablation, Switch-Base with 32 experts.

Full bfloat16 diverged. Full float32 trained but ran 17% slower. Selective precision matched float32's quality at bfloat16's speed — strictly the best of both, which almost never happens.

The trick is where the island ends

Switch casts “the router input to float32 precision”, and critically: “the float32 precision is only used within the body of the router function—on computations local to that device. Because the resulting dispatch and combine tensors are recast to bfloat16 precision at the end of the function, no expensive float32 tensors are broadcast through all-to-all communication operations.”

So the float32 region is deliberately drawn to end before the collective. You get exact softmax arithmetic where the numerics are fragile, and half-width tensors on the wire where the bandwidth is scarce. It is a numerics fix and a communication optimisation in the same three lines.

The inference problem nobody advertises

Now the reckoning that chapter 1's hospital analogy promised: the hospital still pays every doctor's salary.

At inference, a sparse model has the memory footprint of a dense model its total size and the arithmetic of a dense model its active size. Mixtral needs all 46.7B parameters resident, because the router may ask for any expert on the very next token — while doing 12.9B parameters of work.

That combination is precisely the shape of a memory-bandwidth-bound workload. Generating one token at a time means reading enormous weight matrices to perform a small amount of arithmetic per byte read. The GPU is not computing; it is waiting for memory.

Labelled honestly

That last paragraph is an arithmetic-intensity argument, not a line I can quote from one of these papers. It follows from the ratio of bytes moved to FLOPs performed at batch size 1, and it is thoroughly established for dense LLM decoding — MoE inherits and worsens it, because the bytes grow with total parameters while the FLOPs track active ones. But this book distinguishes what a paper says from what follows, and this is the latter.

The related fact I can source is GShard's measurement that dense feed-forward layers hit “>85% peak FLOPS” while attention reaches only >30%, being “bounded by memory bandwidth” — the same wall, measured on the same hardware.

Which yields the honest summary of the entire architecture:

MoE is a bargain that trades memory and network for compute. On a training cluster, where compute is the binding constraint and the interconnect is excellent, that trade is superb — DeepSeek-V3 trained a 671B model for 2.788M H800 GPU-hours, about $5.576M at $2/GPU-hour by the paper's own reckoning. On a single GPU serving one user, where memory is the binding constraint, the same trade is bad.

How the frontier actually serves it

DeepSeek-V3's serving stack is a different machine from its training stack. No tensor parallelism in training at all — “we meticulously optimize the memory footprint… thereby enabling us to train DeepSeek-V3 without using costly Tensor Parallelism” — it trains as PP16 × EP64 × ZeRO-1 DP, and serves as TP4/DP8/EP32 when prefilling and TP4/DP80/EP320 when decoding.

Look at that decode configuration. EP320 — the experts are spread across hundreds of GPUs, so that the aggregate memory bandwidth is large enough to feed one token's worth of work. That is what "memory-bound" looks like when someone with a cluster solves it.

Work it yourself

You want to serve Mixtral 8×7B (46.7B total, 12.9B active) in FP16 on a single A100-80GB.

(a) Does it fit? Show the arithmetic.
(b) A colleague argues that since only 13B parameters are active, you should be able to serve it in roughly the memory of a 13B dense model, loading experts on demand. What's wrong with that?
(c) Using chapter 2's finding that ~24–28% of consecutive tokens reuse the same top-1 expert at layer 15 (against a 12.5% baseline), say whether on-demand loading is hopeless or merely difficult — and what you'd measure to decide.

Show the answer

(a) 46.7B parameters × 2 bytes = 93.4 GB of weights alone, before any KV cache or activations. An 80GB A100 cannot hold it. You need two, or quantisation — which is why Mixtral is so commonly served at 4-bit (~23GB, comfortable).

(b) Two problems. First, which 13B is active changes every token and every layer — the active set isn't a fixed subset you can preload, it's a different 2-of-8 per layer per token. Second, PCIe transfer is roughly an order of magnitude slower than HBM; streaming an expert in to use it once costs far more time than the arithmetic it enables. You'd convert a memory-bound problem into a much worse PCIe-bound one.

(c) Merely difficult, and the locality is why — though the effect is smaller than the headline figure suggests. Top-1 reuse at layer 15 runs 24–28% against a 12.5% baseline: roughly twice chance, not five times. So an expert you just paged in has about a one-in-four chance of being wanted again next token. That is well short of a free lunch, but it is a real signal, and it is what makes an LRU expert cache worth building rather than hopeless.

What you'd measure: the hit rate of a fixed-size expert cache under a real request trace, per layer — because chapter 2's figure is for layer 15 specifically, and locality varied by depth. Then compare the amortised transfer cost per token against the memory you save. The decision is empirical, and the measurement is cheap.

What you can now do

  • Explain expert parallelism and how it differs from tensor and data parallelism.
  • Name the two all-to-alls, and know a training step pays four per MoE layer.
  • Quote what growing from 128 to 2048 experts does to utilisation — and why GShard's O(√D) is topology-specific.
  • Explain why DeepSeek-V3 caps tokens at 4 nodes, in terms of the 3.2× NVLink-to-InfiniBand ratio rather than expert count.
  • Say why Switch's float32 island ends before the collective.
  • Explain why MoE inference is memory-bound — and flag that as an arithmetic-intensity argument rather than a quotation.

Where that leaves us

Six chapters ago, the observation was that a language model does identical arithmetic for “the” and for “photosynthesis.” Everything since has been the consequences of trying to fix that.

You can break the weld between parameters and compute (1), but then you need something to choose, and the choice must be discrete yet differentiable (2). The chooser plays favourites, so you add a loss that watches it (3). The hardware wants fixed shapes, so you add buffers, and tokens fall out of them (4). Seven years of arguing sharpen each piece (5). And none of it fits on one machine, so the network becomes part of the model (6).

Not one of those steps is exotic. Each is the obvious response to the previous one's failure. That's what makes MoE learnable rather than merely memorisable — and it's why, when the next paper lands, you'll be able to read it as a reply to something rather than as a novelty.
