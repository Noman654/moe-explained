The lineage

Seven years of arguing

Every design decision in the previous four chapters was somebody's reaction to something that hurt. Read in order, the papers stop being a reading list and become a conversation — one with at least one genuine reversal in it.

~19 min Assumes: chapters 1–4 One animation, one exercise

You now know how an MoE layer works. What you don't yet know is why it looks like this — why top-2 and not top-5, why 256 tiny experts instead of 8 large ones, why the balancing loss has the exact shape it does. None of it was designed. All of it was argued into existence, and the arguments are recoverable because each paper states what it disliked about its predecessor.

2017 — proving it works at all

Shazeer et al. had to establish something nobody had shown: that conditional computation could work at language-model scale. They bolted sparsely-gated MoE layers between LSTM layers and pushed hard, claiming “greater than 1000x improvements in model capacity with only minor losses in computational efficiency.”

Three things everyone gets wrong about this paper

1 — the 137B is the layer, not the model. The paper says “up to 137 billion parameters in the MoE layer”. Quoting it as a 137B model overstates it by a lot.

2 — that configuration got worse. Verbatim: perplexity “improves significantly up to 65536 experts (68 billion parameters), dropping 39% lower than the computationally matched baseline, but degrades at 131072 experts.” The famous number is past the peak.

3 — top-k was 4, and the big models were hierarchical. “We use Noisy-Top-K Gating with k=4 for the ordinary MoE layers and k=2 at each level of the hierarchical MoE layers.” The 131,072-expert model is a gate over gates, not a flat top-k over 131,072 options.

And buried in section 2.1 is a single sentence that the field would spend four years taking seriously:

“If we choose k > 1, the gate values for the top k experts have nonzero derivatives with respect to the weights of the gating network.”

Note what that sentence actually establishes: k > 1 is sufficient for a trainable router. It contains no argument that k = 1 would fail. But it was read as though it did, and it hardened into received wisdom — you need at least two experts so the router has something to compare. Hold that thought.

2020 — GShard makes it a systems problem

GShard's complaints about 2017 are stated plainly, and they are the two failures you met in chapters 3 and 4. First, imbalance: “most tokens seen during training would have been dispatched to a small number of experts… leaving other experts untrained.” Second — and this is new — parallelism: “a sequential implementation of the gating function would keep most of the computational resources idle.”

GShard is where MoE stops being a modelling idea and becomes a distributed-systems idea. Local group dispatching, expert capacity as a first-class quantity, sharding annotations in the compiler — the entire vocabulary of chapter 4 arrives here.

That comparison is the whole argument for sparsity in one line: the 600B sparse model cost 22.4 core-years; the best dense Transformer they trained (2.3B parameters) cost 235.5 core-years — over ten times more compute for a worse result. And scaling was sublinear: going from 37.5B to 600B, a 16× increase in parameters, raised cost only 3.6×.

GShard's top-2 isn't always two

A detail routinely flattened into “GShard = top-2”: the second expert is dispatched stochastically. Per the paper, the gate “dispatches to the 2nd-best expert with the probability proportional to its weight g2”. When the runner-up's gate is small, the token often goes to one expert only — conserving capacity. GShard's average experts-per-token is therefore strictly less than 2.

2021 — the heresy that turned out to be right

Switch Transformer did the thing everyone knew you couldn't: it routed each token to exactly one expert.

And it named its target precisely. Not GShard — Shazeer, four years earlier:

The rebuttal, verbatim

“Shazeer et al. (2017) conjectured that routing to k > 1 experts was necessary in order to have non-trivial gradients to the routing functions. The authors intuited that learning to route would not work without the ability to compare at least two experts.”

And the refutation, which is one sentence long:

“Note that for both MoE and Switch Routing, the gate value pi(x)… permits differentiability of the router.”

You have already derived this. It is chapter 2, in full: the router's gradient does not come from comparing experts. It comes from the gate value multiplying the expert's output. Even with k = 1 the layer computes pi(x) · Ei(x), so ∂y/∂pi is non-zero and the gradient flows. The comparison was never doing the work.

A four-year-old assumption, resting on one sentence that only ever claimed sufficiency, cost the field a factor of two in expert compute.

Switch's wins from k = 1 are concrete: less router computation, simpler communication, and — the one people forget — expert capacity “can be at least halved since each token is only being routed to a single expert.” Half the buffer, which after chapter 4 you know is half the padding shipped over the network.

The famous number is the weaker model

Switch-C is the one everyone quotes: 1571B parameters (rounded to “1.6T” in prose), 2048 experts. Switch-XXL is 395B, 64 experts. Switch-XXL beats Switch-C on negative log perplexity at both 250k and 500k steps (−1.086/−1.008 vs −1.096/−1.043) and on SQuAD (89.6 vs 87.7) — while having about four times fewer parameters.

The paper's explanation: Switch-XXL “applies ~10x the FLOPS per token than the Switch-C model, even though it has ~4x less unique parameters.” Expert count alone was already hitting diminishing returns in 2021. Which is exactly the gap the next paper attacks — from the opposite direction.

2024 — Mixtral opens the door

By 2023 every frontier lab was rumoured to be running MoE and nobody outside one could inspect a serious sparse model. Mixtral 8×7B's contribution isn't an architectural idea — it's 8 experts, top-2, the GShard shape — it's that the weights shipped under Apache 2.0. Everything in chapter 1 is derivable because Mixtral published enough to derive it.

Mixtral's name is arithmetic bait

“8×7B” suggests 56B parameters. The model is 46.7B. And 2×7B suggests 14B active; it's 12.9B.

The reason is the one you computed in chapter 1: only the FFN blocks are replicated. Attention, embeddings and norms are shared across all eight experts, so they're counted once, not eight times. The paper states the architecture and states both numbers, but never spells out the reconciliation — that arithmetic is inference, which is why chapter 1 walked you through it rather than quoting it.

The combinatorial argument

DeepSeekMoE's complaint is that conventional MoE suffers from knowledge hybridity — with only 8 or 16 experts, each one is forced to absorb a wide, unrelated mix of things, because there simply aren't enough of them to specialise.

Their fix is to cut each expert into m smaller ones and activate m times as many. The FLOPs are identical. What changes is how many distinct combinations of experts a token can reach:

DeepSeekMoE, §3.1
C(16, 2) = 120    →    C(64, 8) = 4,426,165,368
Sixteen experts choose two gives 120 possible routings. Split each into four and pick eight of the resulting 64, for identical compute, and you get 4.4 billion. That is the argument for fine-grained experts, in one line.
How much to believe it

Two honest caveats, both from the paper itself.

First, this is an illustration, not DeepSeekMoE's configuration. The shipped 16B model has 64 routed experts plus 2 shared, activating 6 of 64 — don't report 4.4 billion as a property of a real model.

Second, the paper's own framing is modest: fine-grained segmentation “substantially enhances the combinatorial flexibility”. It never claims the model uses 4.4 billion configurations, and reachable states are not the same as useful capacity — most combinations are never visited. The real evidence is an ablation: disable the top routed experts and DeepSeekMoE degrades faster than a GShard baseline with 1.5× the parameters, indicating less redundancy — each expert is carrying something the others aren't. That result, not the binomial, is the load-bearing argument.

And granularity has a floor, which the paper is candid about: they did not go finer at 16B “due to the potential reduction in computational efficiency associated with excessively small expert sizes”. Tiny matrices make bad GPU kernels. The combinatorial argument is bounded by hardware — and duly, once the scale justified it, DeepSeek-V3 went to 256 routed experts.

Shared experts, and the thing they don't have

The second idea: alongside the routed experts, a small number of shared experts that process every token. The logic follows from the first idea. If you want routed experts to specialise, stop making them relearn the common cases — grammar, punctuation, whatever every token needs. Hand that to an always-on expert and free the specialists to be specialists.

Shared experts have no gate

In the FFN equation, the shared-expert term is a plain sum — Σ FFNi(u) — with no gi,t factor. Their weight is implicitly 1 and the router never sees them at all.

So diagrams drawing shared experts as “routed experts that always win” are wrong. They aren't in the competition. Architecturally they behave like part of the dense backbone — which, as chapter 1's exercise showed, is exactly why they drag DeepSeek-V3's sparsity ratio down.

DeepSeekMoE credits the mechanism honestly, too: “the prototype of shared expert isolation can be credited to Rajbhandari et al.… The key distinction lies in the fact that they derive this strategy from an engineering perspective, while we approach it from an algorithmic standpoint.” The claimed contribution is the rationale, not the trick.

Two corrections carried forward

DeepSeek-V3 is not auxiliary-loss-free. As chapter 3 noted, it keeps a complementary sequence-wise balance loss. The name describes the primary mechanism, not the absence of all auxiliary losses.

V3 changed the gating function. DeepSeekMoE used a softmax; V3 “uses the sigmoid function to compute the affinity scores, and applies a normalization among all selected affinity scores.” Carrying the softmax formula forward from DeepSeekMoE into V3 is a genuine error — and it interacts with the bias trick, because sigmoid scores are independent per expert, so adding bi doesn't implicitly rescale the others the way it would inside a softmax.

Work it yourself

(a) Switch-XXL (395B, 64 experts) outperformed Switch-C (1571B, 2048 experts) on both perplexity and SQuAD. Using what you know from chapters 1 and 4, give two distinct reasons more experts can make a model worse at fixed training budget.
(b) DeepSeekMoE splits 16 experts into 64 and activates 8 instead of 2. Verify that the FLOPs are unchanged, and state what quantity actually increased.
(c) Given the combinatorial argument, why doesn't anyone ship 65,536 experts of 1/4096th size each?

Show the answer

(a) Two reasons, both already in your hands. First, FLOPs per token. The paper's own explanation is that Switch-XXL “applies ~10x the FLOPS per token” — parameters you never compute with don't help, and chapter 1's distinction between stored and active is exactly this. Second, tokens per expert. At a fixed token budget, 2048 experts each see ~1/32nd of what 64 experts see. Each is trained on far less data and stays closer to initialisation — the same starvation dynamic as chapter 3's collapse, arriving through arithmetic rather than through a greedy router.

(b) Each of the 64 experts is 1/4 the size of one of the original 16. Activating 8 of them costs 8 × (1/4) = 2 expert-units, identical to the original 2 × 1 = 2. Total parameters are also unchanged: 64 × (1/4) = 16. What increased is neither compute nor capacity but reachable combinations — from C(16,2) = 120 to C(64,8) = 4,426,165,368.

(c) Kernel efficiency, and the paper says so: finer segmentation is avoided “due to the potential reduction in computational efficiency associated with excessively small expert sizes.” A matrix multiply on a tiny matrix wastes most of a GPU — you become launch- and memory-bound rather than compute-bound, and the FLOPs you saved on paper you lose to utilisation. There's also a routing cost: the router matrix is d_model × N, so N = 65,536 makes the router itself enormous, and a top-k over 65,536 candidates per token per layer stops being free. The combinatorial argument is real but it is bounded by hardware — which is why DeepSeek went to 256, not 65,536.

What you can now do

  • Name what each paper was reacting to, in its own words.
  • Explain why Switch's top-1 was considered impossible and why it wasn't — using the gradient argument from chapter 2.
  • Avoid three common misquotes: Shazeer's 137B (layer, not model, and past the peak), Switch's 1.6T (the weaker of its two big models), and Mixtral's “8×7B” (46.7B, not 56B).
  • State the combinatorial argument for fine-grained experts — and the two reasons to hold it loosely.
  • Explain why shared experts have no gate value.

What this chain never had to confront, chapter by chapter, is that none of these models fit on one accelerator. Every design decision above was made in the presence of a network — and the moment experts live on different devices, routing stops being arithmetic and becomes a communication problem.
